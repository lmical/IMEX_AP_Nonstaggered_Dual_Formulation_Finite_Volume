{\sc\underline{Reply}}: We believe the reviewer is referring to Example 3 (Baroclinic Vorticity Generation), as the density is constant in Example 4.
%
Indeed, in such a test, a visible density jump is present at $y=4$ in the initial condition. This density discontinuity, however, is not accompanied by a corresponding pressure discontinuity. %

Instead, this interaction generates vorticity and leads to the formation of shear-layer instabilities. Consequently, although a sharp density interface is initially present, one should not expect the appearance of strong wave structures at later times.

To avoid possible confusion, we have added a short clarification in the revised manuscript.

\bigskip
\underline{Response to the Comments of Reviewer \#4}


{\bf The manuscript tackles a longstanding challenge in computational fluid dynamics: the severe stiffness of the compressible Euler equations in low-Mach-number regimes due to fast acoustic waves. The authors argue that constructing a purely conservative hyperbolic flux splitting for the full Euler system is highly challenging. To address this, they propose a Dual Formulation approach. Rather than relying on conservative splitting, the method isolates the stiff acoustic component using primitive (non-conservative) variables. To preserve shock-capturing properties, a standard conservative scheme is evolved in parallel, and the two solutions are blended at the end of each time step according to the local Mach number.
	
The theoretical strengths of the paper are noteworthy. The scheme achieves a physically consistent incompressible limit: in the zero-Mach regime, the velocity field becomes divergence-free, and the method correctly derives and solves the corresponding Poisson equation for pressure. Furthermore, the authors provide rigorous asymptotic-preserving proofs for both the first-order and second-order (DeC) semi-implicit time discretizations, which hold under suitable idealized boundary conditions.
	
}

\smallskip
{\sc\underline{Reply}}: We thank the referee for appreciating our work.

\medskip

\begin{itemize}[wide, labelwidth=!, labelindent=0pt]
	\item	
	{\bf
		However, these advantages come with computational and practical trade-offs.
		
		 Evolving two complete sets of variables leads to roughly double the memory requirements and substantially higher computational cost per time step (two Poisson problems per step).

	}
	
	\smallskip
	{\sc\underline{Reply}}: We understand the concern of the referee. Indeed, there is an overhead, under this specific point of view, with respect to schemes evolving a single set of variables.
	%
	However, we would like to point out that this does not strictly correspond to a double computational cost. For example, the reconstructed interface values are used for both $\uvec{V}$- and $\uvec{U}$-solutions, without need for a separate reconstruction.
	%
	Moreover, the evolution of the $\uvec{U}$-solution is much cheaper than the evolution of the $\uvec{V}$-solution, as it is performed with an explicit scheme.
	%
	We have added a remark on this at the end of Section 3.4.
	
	\item		
	\medskip
	{\bf  Additionally, the computation of characteristic wave speeds relies on global quantities $\rho_{\max}$ and $p_{\min}$ (the maximum density and minimum pressure over the entire domain). In large-scale parallel 3D simulations, this introduces frequent global reductions, creating a notable communication bottleneck.}
	
	\smallskip
	{\sc\underline{Reply}}: We thank the reviewer for this observation. The proposed method indeed requires the computation of the global quantities $\rho_{\max}$ and $p_{\min}$. This operation only involves two scalar reductions and therefore represents a relatively small overhead compared with the overall cost of the algorithm, which is dominated by the solution of the linear systems arising from the pressure equation. Nevertheless, we agree that any global communication may affect scalability in extremely large-scale simulations.
	
	We have added a reference to this point in the remark of the previous point.
	
	
	
	\medskip
	\item	
	{\bf 
		
		 To prevent wave-speed collapse in higher-Mach regions, the authors introduce an ad-hoc $\epsilon^4$ regularization. While this modification vanishes in the low-Mach limit, it remains an artificial fix.
		The method also employs a heuristic switching function $s(\epsilon)$ with empirically tuned parameters ($\epsilon_0 = 0.15$, $\epsilon_1 = 0.4$, $\alpha = 14$) to blend the primitive and conservative solution, across all the numerical illustrations. Room for deeper mathematical understanding or Machine Learing is ok here ?
		
	}
	
	\smallskip
	{\sc\underline{Reply}}: We thank the reviewer for this observation.
	
	Concerning the $\varepsilon^4$-modification of the wave speeds, we found it necessary to improve the handling of discontinuous solutions in large (intermediate)-Mach-number regimes. %However, we would like to emphasize that it vanishes asymptotically as $\varepsilon\to0$ and therefore does not affect either the consistency of the method or the asymptotic-preserving analysis presented in the paper.
	
	Regarding the switching function $s(\varepsilon)$, we agree that the adopted parameters have been selected empirically. Nevertheless, we would like to stress that the wide range of numerical experiments reported in the manuscript indicates that the proposed choice is sufficiently robust.
	%
	We agree that a deeper theoretical understanding of the optimal design of the switching function and its parameters would be highly desirable. 
	
	
	These aspects are now discussed in two new remarks at the beginning of Section 4.
